# QAOA from First Principles: Cost, Mixing and Interference

QAOA is often summarised in one sentence: alternate a cost Hamiltonian and a mixer Hamiltonian, then optimise the angles. The summary is correct, but it leaves the mechanism obscure. Why should applying phases help an optimisation problem? Why is a mixer required? Why can the expectation value improve even though every individual circuit operation is unitary? And why does a low expected energy not necessarily mean that the optimal bitstring will be observed frequently?

The useful way to understand QAOA is to follow amplitude through the circuit. The cost unitary does not directly make good solutions more probable. It writes information about objective values into phases. The mixer then converts phase differences into amplitude differences. Repetition allows constructive and destructive interference to reshape the probability distribution.

That is the core idea. The remaining details determine whether it works well on a particular problem.

## 1. The problem Hamiltonian

Start with a classical cost function

$$
C:\{0,1\}^n\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.
$$

Construct a diagonal Hamiltonian $H_C$ satisfying

$$
H_C|z\rangle=C(z)|z\rangle.
$$

For a QUBO, it has the Ising form

$$
H_C=\alpha I+\sum_i h_iZ_i+\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}Z_iZ_j.
$$

The identity term may be omitted from the circuit because it contributes only a global phase. The remaining terms commute with one another:

$$
[Z_i,Z_j]=0,
\qquad
[Z_i,Z_jZ_k]=0,
\qquad
[Z_iZ_j,Z_kZ_\ell]=0.
$$

This makes the cost evolution particularly convenient:

$$
U_C(\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma H_C}.
$$

Since the terms commute,

$$
U_C(\gamma)
=\prod_i e^{-i\gamma h_iZ_i}
\prod_{i<j}e^{-i\gamma J_{ij}Z_iZ_j},
$$

apart from the global phase generated by $\alpha I$.

On a basis state,

$$
U_C(\gamma)|z\rangle
=e^{-i\gamma C(z)}|z\rangle.
$$

The bitstring does not change. Only its phase changes.

## 2. Why phase separation is not enough

Prepare the uniform superposition

$$
|+\rangle^{\otimes n}
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}
\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^n}|z\rangle.
$$

After cost evolution,

$$
U_C(\gamma)|+\rangle^{\otimes n}
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}
\sum_z e^{-i\gamma C(z)}|z\rangle.
$$

Every amplitude still has magnitude $1/\sqrt{2^n}$. Measurement probabilities are therefore unchanged:

$$
\left|\frac{e^{-i\gamma C(z)}}{\sqrt{2^n}}\right|^2
=\frac{1}{2^n}.
$$

The cost unitary has encoded objective information, but that information is invisible to computational-basis measurement. A second operation must make the phased components interact.

## 3. The standard mixer

The standard QAOA mixer Hamiltonian is

$$
H_B=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i,
$$

with unitary

$$
U_B(\beta)=e^{-i\beta H_B}.
$$

Since $X_i$ terms on different qubits commute,

$$
U_B(\beta)=\prod_i e^{-i\beta X_i}.
$$

For one qubit,

$$
e^{-i\beta X}
=\cos\beta\,I-i\sin\beta\,X.
$$

Because $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$ and $X|1\rangle=|0\rangle$, this unitary mixes the amplitudes of basis states that differ by one bit. For $n$ qubits, the standard mixer moves amplitude along edges of the $n$-dimensional hypercube of bitstrings.

The mixer is not random motion. Its effect depends on the relative phases produced by $U_C(\gamma)$. Contributions arriving at a bitstring can add constructively or cancel destructively.

## 4. Noncommutation is the source of nontrivial dynamics

The cost Hamiltonian is diagonal in the computational basis. The standard mixer is diagonal in the $X$ basis. They do not generally commute:

$$
[H_C,H_B]\neq0.
$$

For one term,

$$
[Z,X]=2iY.
$$

If $H_C$ and $H_B$ did commute, alternating them would collapse into a single evolution:

$$
e^{-i\beta H_B}e^{-i\gamma H_C}
=e^{-i(\beta H_B+\gamma H_C)}.
$$

The order would not matter, and the ansatz would lose much of its expressive structure. Noncommutation means that successive layers generate new operator directions through commutators. This is also the point at which QAOA connects with dynamical Lie algebra: the operators generated by repeated commutation of $iH_C$ and $iH_B$ describe the continuous transformations reachable from these controls, subject to symmetry and parameterisation restrictions.

One should be careful with the conclusion. A large dynamical Lie algebra does not automatically imply easy trainability or good low-depth approximation. Controllability describes what may be reachable in principle under sufficiently general control. QAOA uses a highly structured alternating schedule with only $2p$ parameters at depth $p$.

## 5. The QAOA state

At depth $p$, define parameter vectors

$$
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_p),
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_p).
$$

The state is

$$
|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}\rangle
=
U_B(\beta_p)U_C(\gamma_p)
\cdots
U_B(\beta_1)U_C(\gamma_1)
|+\rangle^{\otimes n}.
$$

The classical outer loop minimises the expected cost

$$
F_p(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
=
\langle\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|
H_C
|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}\rangle.
$$

If the final state is

$$
|\psi\rangle=\sum_z a_z|z\rangle,
$$

then, because $H_C$ is diagonal,

$$
F_p=\sum_z|a_z|^2C(z).
$$

QAOA is therefore optimising an average over the output distribution. It is not directly optimising the cost of the single most likely bitstring or the probability of the exact optimum.

## 6. A one-qubit calculation

Consider the simple problem

$$
\min_{x\in\{0,1\}} x.
$$

The optimal bit is $x=0$. The cost Hamiltonian is

$$
H_C=\frac{I-Z}{2}.
$$

Ignoring the identity term in the circuit, use

$$
\widetilde{H}_C=-\frac{1}{2}Z.
$$

At $p=1$,

$$
|\gamma,\beta\rangle
=e^{-i\beta X}e^{+i\gamma Z/2}|+\rangle.
$$

The cost unitary creates a relative phase between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. The $X$ rotation then converts that relative phase into a difference in measurement probabilities. A direct Bloch-sphere calculation gives

$$
\langle Z\rangle=-\sin(2\beta)\sin\gamma
$$

under this sign convention, and therefore

$$
\langle H_C\rangle
=\frac{1-\langle Z\rangle}{2}
=\frac{1+\sin(2\beta)\sin\gamma}{2}.
$$

Choosing angles so that $\sin(2\beta)\sin\gamma=-1$ gives zero expected cost and prepares $|0\rangle$ exactly. The example is trivial as an optimisation problem, but it isolates the mechanism: cost-dependent phase, followed by mixing, changes probability.

## 7. QAOA for MaxCut

For a graph $G=(V,E)$, assign a binary variable $x_i$ to each vertex. An edge $(i,j)$ is cut when $x_i\neq x_j$. Its contribution is

$$
x_i+x_j-2x_ix_j.
$$

Using spins, the same indicator is

$$
\frac{1-Z_iZ_j}{2}.
$$

The cut-value operator is

$$
H_{\text{cut}}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\frac{I-Z_iZ_j}{2}.
$$

If we use a minimum-eigensolver, take $H_C=-H_{\text{cut}}$. Alternatively, retain $H_{\text{cut}}$ and maximise its expectation. The original QAOA paper analysed this problem and showed explicitly how performance depends on depth and local graph structure.

MaxCut is useful because the encoding is clean, exact solutions are available for small graphs, and graph families can be varied systematically. It is a benchmark, not evidence that every financial QUBO will behave similarly.

## 8. Building a QAOA circuit in Qiskit

The code below constructs the ansatz explicitly for an Ising Hamiltonian

$$
\widetilde H_C=\sum_i h_iZ_i+\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}Z_iZ_j.
$$

Qiskit's `rz(theta)` implements $e^{-i\theta Z/2}$, so $e^{-i\gamma h_iZ_i}$ requires `rz(2 * gamma * h_i)`. A CNOT–$R_Z$–CNOT sequence implements a $ZZ$ rotation.

```python
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.circuit import ParameterVector

def qaoa_circuit(n, h, J, p):
    gamma = ParameterVector("gamma", p)
    beta = ParameterVector("beta", p)

    qc = QuantumCircuit(n)
    qc.h(range(n))

    for layer in range(p):
        # Cost evolution: exp(-i gamma H_C)
        for i, coeff in h.items():
            qc.rz(2 * gamma[layer] * coeff, i)

        for (i, j), coeff in J.items():
            qc.cx(i, j)
            qc.rz(2 * gamma[layer] * coeff, j)
            qc.cx(i, j)

        # Mixer evolution: exp(-i beta sum_i X_i)
        for i in range(n):
            qc.rx(2 * beta[layer], i)

    return qc, gamma, beta
```

This is worth writing once even if a library later generates the ansatz. It exposes the relationship between Hamiltonian coefficients and gate angles.

## 9. Exact expectations before shot noise

For small instances, use a statevector to evaluate the objective exactly. This separates ansatz behaviour from sampling noise and device noise.

```python
import numpy as np
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector

def classical_energy_from_index(index, n, alpha, h, J):
    bits = [(index >> i) & 1 for i in range(n)]
    spins = [1 - 2 * bit for bit in bits]
    value = alpha
    value += sum(coeff * spins[i] for i, coeff in h.items())
    value += sum(coeff * spins[i] * spins[j]
                 for (i, j), coeff in J.items())
    return value

def expected_energy(parameter_values, circuit, parameters,
                    n, alpha, h, J):
    bound = circuit.assign_parameters(dict(zip(parameters, parameter_values)))
    probabilities = Statevector.from_instruction(bound).probabilities()
    energies = np.array([
        classical_energy_from_index(k, n, alpha, h, J)
        for k in range(2**n)
    ])
    return float(probabilities @ energies)
```

Optimise with a classical routine:

```python
from scipy.optimize import minimize

n = 4
p = 1
alpha = 0.0
h = {0: -0.5, 1: 0.25, 2: -0.1, 3: 0.4}
J = {(0, 1): 0.3, (1, 2): -0.2, (2, 3): 0.35}

circuit, gamma, beta = qaoa_circuit(n, h, J, p)
parameters = list(gamma) + list(beta)

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
initial = rng.uniform(0, np.pi, size=2 * p)

result = minimize(
    expected_energy,
    x0=initial,
    args=(circuit, parameters, n, alpha, h, J),
    method="COBYLA",
    options={"maxiter": 500},
)

print(result.fun)
print(result.x)
```

One initialisation is not a benchmark. Repeat the optimisation from many starting points and report the distribution of final energies, objective evaluations, and success probabilities.

## 10. Reading the final distribution

After optimisation, inspect the full probability distribution.

```python
bound = circuit.assign_parameters(dict(zip(parameters, result.x)))
probabilities = Statevector.from_instruction(bound).probabilities()

rows = []
for index, probability in enumerate(probabilities):
    bits_qiskit = format(index, f"0{n}b")
    energy = classical_energy_from_index(index, n, alpha, h, J)
    rows.append((bits_qiskit, probability, energy))

for bitstring, probability, energy in sorted(
    rows, key=lambda row: row[1], reverse=True
)[:10]:
    print(bitstring, probability, energy)
```

Three outputs should be kept distinct:

1. The expectation $\langle H_C\rangle$.
2. The most probable sampled bitstring.
3. The best bitstring obtained after a specified number of shots.

They need not coincide. A distribution can have a good expectation while spreading probability across several near-optimal strings. It can also have a mediocre expectation while assigning a small but nonzero probability to the optimum.

## 11. What increasing depth changes

At depth $p$, QAOA has $2p$ scalar angles in its standard form. Greater depth can enlarge the accessible family of states and, in ideal theory, the best achievable objective cannot worsen if the shallower circuit can be embedded in the deeper family.

Practical performance may still deteriorate because:

- the classical parameter landscape becomes harder to search;
- circuit depth increases exposure to noise;
- transpilation introduces additional two-qubit gates;
- each objective evaluation becomes more expensive;
- finite-shot estimates make the landscape noisy;
- a fixed optimisation budget is spread over more parameters.

The statement “larger $p$ is better” refers to the optimum over parameters in an ideal ansatz, not necessarily to the output of a finite classical optimisation run on noisy hardware.

## 12. Parameter landscapes and symmetries

The parameters are periodic because they generate unitary rotations. Exact periods depend on the Hamiltonian spectrum and coefficient normalisation. Searching a large unrestricted domain wastes evaluations and can duplicate physically equivalent circuits.

Graph symmetries, global bit-flip symmetry, and parameter symmetries can further reduce the distinct landscape. They can also produce degenerate optima. A benchmark should state:

- the parameter domain;
- the coefficient scaling;
- the initialisation rule;
- the optimiser and stopping condition;
- the number of restarts;
- whether parameters are transferred from smaller depth or nearby instances.

Without these details, “QAOA at $p=3$” is not a reproducible method.

## 13. The classical optimiser is part of QAOA

QAOA is a hybrid algorithm. Its performance cannot be attributed only to the quantum circuit. The classical outer loop chooses the next parameters using noisy or exact estimates of $F_p$.

Derivative-free methods such as COBYLA are common for small studies, but their behaviour depends on constraints, scaling, and evaluation budget. SPSA can tolerate noisy objectives but introduces its own hyperparameters. Gradient-based optimisation can use parameter-shift or analytic structure, but the cost of estimating gradients must be counted.

For every optimiser, record:

- total circuit evaluations;
- total shots;
- convergence history;
- final expectation;
- final success probability;
- variation across random starts.

A favourable result from one carefully chosen seed is not evidence of robust performance.

## 14. Noise enters at several levels

There is no single “QAOA noise.” At least four effects should be separated.

### 14.1 Shot noise

With a finite number of measurements, an expectation value is estimated rather than known. Its variance decreases on the order of $1/N$ with the number of shots $N$, so the standard error decreases as $1/\sqrt{N}$.

### 14.2 Gate noise and decoherence

Imperfect one- and two-qubit gates, relaxation, and dephasing change the prepared state. Two-qubit gates are particularly important because a dense Ising Hamiltonian can require many $ZZ$ interactions.

### 14.3 Readout error

The observed classical bitstring can differ from the measured quantum state. This directly distorts feasibility and success probabilities.

### 14.4 Optimisation noise

The classical optimiser sees noisy function values. It may stop early, follow a misleading direction, or fail to distinguish nearby parameter points.

These effects interact. A deeper circuit may be more expressive but produce a less reliable objective estimate. Hardware experiments should therefore be compared with noiseless statevector results and controlled noisy simulations on the same instance.

## 15. Constraints change the mixer

The standard $X$ mixer connects every bitstring to strings at Hamming distance one. If the problem requires exactly $K$ selected assets, a single bit flip leaves the feasible subspace. A penalty can discourage these states, but the circuit still allocates amplitude to them.

An alternative is an $XY$-type mixer, with terms such as

$$
H_{XY}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E_M}(X_iX_j+Y_iY_j).
$$

This evolution exchanges excitations between qubits and preserves Hamming weight. Starting from a state with exactly $K$ ones, the circuit remains in the cardinality-$K$ subspace.

The design requirements are stricter than “preserve feasibility.” The mixer should also connect the feasible solutions sufficiently well. If the feasible-state graph is disconnected, the circuit cannot reach some valid solutions from the chosen initial state.

This is why constrained QAOA is a modelling problem as much as a circuit problem. The feasible set determines the geometry through which amplitude is allowed to move.

## 16. QAOA and the adiabatic picture

QAOA is often motivated as a discretised form of quantum annealing or adiabatic evolution. Begin with a mixer Hamiltonian whose ground state is easy to prepare, then gradually change from the mixer to the problem Hamiltonian:

$$
H(s)=(1-s)H_B+sH_C,
\qquad s\in[0,1].
$$

In an ideal adiabatic process, sufficiently slow evolution can keep the system near the instantaneous ground state. QAOA replaces a continuous schedule with alternating finite evolutions under $H_B$ and $H_C$. At large depth, appropriately chosen angles can approximate such a schedule.

But low-depth QAOA should not be understood only as crude annealing. Its angles are variational and may exploit schedules that are not monotonic or adiabatic. The circuit can be analysed as its own ansatz.

## 17. What would count as understanding QAOA?

Running a library implementation is useful, but it is not the end point. A complete understanding should allow us to answer:

- Which classical objective is encoded in $H_C$?
- Why does the cost unitary change phase but not probability?
- Which basis states does the mixer connect?
- Does the mixer preserve the constraints?
- Why must the cost and mixer fail to commute?
- What quantity is the classical optimiser minimising?
- How does that quantity differ from success probability?
- Which computational costs belong to the quantum loop and which to the classical loop?
- How do depth, shots, noise, and initialisation change the result?

QAOA is simple enough to write in one equation and complicated enough that every component can become a research problem. Its value lies in that structure. It converts a classical cost landscape into quantum phases, uses noncommuting dynamics to turn those phases into a new probability distribution, and exposes the result to classical optimisation. Whether this produces a useful algorithm is not guaranteed by the construction. It must be measured.
